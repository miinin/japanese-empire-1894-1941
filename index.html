The Japanese Empire 1894 – 1941

From the war with Qing China to the attack on Pearl Harbor: how a state that had been forced open by American warships in 1853 built an empire in Asia, and why, 88 years later, it chose war with the United States.

    What you should be able to do after this lesson

    Each task is labelled with the goals it practises, for example G2. In the exit ticket you rate yourself against these four goals.

    1. G1Chronology. Place the key events of Japanese expansion, 1894–1941, in order and explain how each one led to the next.
    2. G2Causation. Explain the political, economic, ideological and strategic causes of expansion, and judge which mattered most in 1931 and in 1941.
    3. G3International response. Explain why the League of Nations and the Western powers failed to stop Japan, and why the US used economic pressure.
    4. G4Sources and perspectives. Analyse primary sources for origin, purpose and content, and use historians’ interpretations in an argument.

    Where this fits in the IB course

    Paper 1 Prescribed subject: The move to global war, case study 1: Japanese expansion in East Asia, 1931–1941.

    Paper 2 World history topic: Causes and effects of 20th-century wars (the Second World War in the Pacific).

    HL Paper 3 History of Asia and Oceania: Japan’s modernization and expansion.

    The years 1894–1930 are background. The examined case study starts in 1931, but you cannot explain 1931 without them.

    The big picture in 60 seconds Support: start here

    • 1894–1912: Japan wins wars against China (1895) and Russia (1905) and takes Taiwan and Korea. It is the first non-Western power to defeat a European great power in modern times.
    • 1912–1930: Japan joins the Western system after the First World War, but feels cheated and disrespected by it. Politics at home becomes more democratic, and also more unstable.
    • 1931–1936: The Depression hits. The army in Manchuria acts on its own and creates Manchukuo. Japan leaves the League of Nations. Assassinations push civilian politicians aside.
    • 1937–1941: Full war with China becomes a stalemate. Japan needs resources, so it moves south. The US answers with an oil embargo, and Japan decides to strike first.

    03 · Causation · G2 G3

    Why did Japan expand?

    Key idea: no single cause explains expansion. The causes connected, and their weight changed between 1931 and 1941.

    Strong IB answers do not list causes. They weigh them and show how they connect. Each card ends with a counter-argument for you to use.

    Strategic

    Security and the “line of advantage”

    Meiji leaders believed Japan would be colonised unless it became strong. In 1890 Prime Minister Yamagata Aritomo argued that Japan had to defend not only its borders (the “line of sovereignty”) but also a wider zone around them (the “line of advantage”). Korea, and later Manchuria, were treated as that buffer against Russia and, after 1917, against Soviet communism.

    But: by 1937 Japan was attacking China itself, and in 1941 it went to war with the strongest economy on earth. Security does not explain the scale of these decisions.

    Economic

    Resources, markets and the Depression

    Japan had little coal, iron or oil and a fast-growing population. The 1929 crash ruined the silk export trade, and rural incomes collapsed. Manchuria offered coal, iron, soybeans and land for settlers. The army’s planners wanted autarky: an empire that could fight a long “total war” without imports.

    But: Manchukuo cost more than it earned for years, and the China war made Japan more dependent on American oil and scrap metal, not less.

    Political

    A constitution that let the military act alone

    Under the Meiji Constitution (1889) the army and navy answered to the Emperor, not to the cabinet (the “right of supreme command”). The service ministers had to be serving officers, so the army could bring down a government by withdrawing its minister. In Manchuria the Kwantung Army created facts on the ground and Tokyo accepted them.

    But: Tokyo approved most of these moves afterwards, and the public and press cheered them. The army was not acting against the nation’s will.

    Ideological

    Emperor, nation and “Asia for the Asians”

    Schools taught loyalty to the Emperor and the uniqueness of the national polity (kokutai). Pan-Asianists said Japan had a mission to free Asia from Western rule. Slogans such as hakkō ichiu (“the eight corners of the world under one roof”) and the “Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere” (1940) presented conquest as liberation.

    But: Japanese rule in Korea, China and later Southeast Asia was often brutal and exploitative. Ideology justified expansion, but did it cause it?

    International

    A hypocritical and weak world order

    Japan copied Western imperialism, then was told the age of empire was over. The Triple Intervention (1895), the rejected racial equality clause at Paris (1919), the 5:5:3 naval ratio (1922) and the US ban on Japanese immigration (1924) all looked like discrimination. When Japan took Manchuria, the League of Nations protested but did nothing.

    But: grievance explains anger, not policy. Many Japanese diplomats, like Foreign Minister Shidehara Kijūrō in the 1920s, chose cooperation with the West in the same conditions.

    Contingency

    Momentum and opportunism

    Many key steps were not planned in Tokyo: a bomb on a railway in 1931, a shooting at the Marco Polo Bridge in 1937. Each success made retreat harder, because withdrawing would mean admitting that soldiers had died for nothing. Germany’s victories in Europe in 1940 left French, Dutch and British colonies almost undefended.

    But: opportunities only matter if someone wants to take them. Momentum needs a direction.

    Strike North or Strike South?

    Japanese strategy split two ways. The army favoured Hokushin-ron (“northern advance”): fight the Soviet Union in Siberia and Mongolia. The navy favoured Nanshin-ron (“southern advance”): take the oil, rubber and tin of Southeast Asia.

    The north lost the argument at Nomonhan (Khalkhin Gol, May–September 1939), where Soviet forces under Georgy Zhukov defeated the Japanese army. In April 1941 Japan signed a neutrality pact with the USSR. The road south lay open, and the US Navy stood on that road.

    Army against army: 1936

    The army itself was divided. The Kōdōha (Imperial Way faction) were young radicals who wanted a “Shōwa Restoration” through direct action. The Tōseiha (Control faction) wanted to prepare the whole economy for total war through the state bureaucracy.

    On 26 February 1936 about 1,400 Kōdōha-led troops occupied central Tokyo and murdered senior ministers. The Emperor ordered the revolt crushed. The winners were the Tōseiha, who now dominated a frightened civilian government.

    Suomi-yhteys · The Finland connection

    A Japanese colonel and Finnish activists, 1904–1905

    During the Russo-Japanese War, Colonel Akashi Motojirō, Japan’s military attaché in Europe, secretly funded revolutionaries inside the Russian Empire to weaken the Tsar from within. His main partner was the Finnish activist Konni Zilliacus. Japanese money helped buy the rifles shipped on the steamer John Grafton in 1905, which ran aground in the Gulf of Bothnia. For many Finns, Japan’s victory over Russia was a sign of hope; the Great Strike in Finland followed in the autumn of 1905.

    Discuss with a partner: does it change how we judge Japanese imperialism if the same state was seen as a liberator by some peoples ruled by Russia, while it was colonising Korea?

    Then and now

    Three questions that are still alive

    Do sanctions deter or provoke?

    The 1941 oil embargo was meant to stop Japan. It pushed Japan’s leaders towards war instead. Since 2022 the EU, including Finland, has used large-scale sanctions against Russia. What conditions make sanctions work?

    Energy dependence

    Japan’s weakness was that it bought most of its oil from a possible enemy. In 2022 Finland stopped importing Russian gas and electricity. Why do states treat energy as a security question?

    History in today’s politics

    School textbooks, the Yasukuni Shrine and the “comfort women” issue still strain Japan’s relations with China and South Korea. Why is this history harder to “close” than, for example, the Russo-Japanese War?

    04 · 1940–1941 · G2 G3

    The road to Pearl Harbor: the oil trap

    Key idea: every Japanese move south brought new US sanctions, and every sanction made the move south look more necessary.

    In 1940 Japan bought most of its oil from the United States; the figure usually given is about 80 percent. Its stockpile would last about two years at most.

    Action and reaction

    Eighteen months from pressure to war

    1. Jan 1940The 1911 US–Japan commercial treaty expires; the US is free to restrict trade.
    2. Sep 1940Japan occupies northern French Indochina to cut supplies to China. The US bans exports of scrap iron and steel. Japan signs the Tripartite Pact with Germany and Italy (27 Sept).
    3. Apr 1941Soviet–Japanese Neutrality Pact. The northern option is closed; talks between Hull and Ambassador Nomura begin in Washington.
    4. Jul 1941An Imperial Conference (2 July) decides to advance south even at the risk of war. Japan occupies southern Indochina. The US freezes Japanese assets (26 July); Britain and the Netherlands follow.
    5. Aug 1941The United States stops all oil exports to Japan. In Tokyo this is called “ABCD encirclement” (America, Britain, China, Dutch).
    6. Sep–Oct 1941The Imperial Conference of 6 September sets a deadline for diplomacy. Prime Minister Konoe cannot get the army to accept withdrawal from China and resigns; General Tōjō Hideki becomes prime minister (18 Oct).
    7. 26 Nov 1941US Secretary of State Cordell Hull’s note demands withdrawal from China and Indochina. The same day the carrier strike force leaves the Kuril Islands.
    8. 7/8 Dec 1941Attacks on Pearl Harbor, Malaya, the Philippines, Hong Kong, Guam and Wake. At Pearl Harbor 2,403 Americans die; the US carriers are at sea and survive.

    Why attack the US at all?

    Japan’s target was the oil of the Dutch East Indies. Planners believed the US would not stand aside, and the US Pacific Fleet at Pearl Harbor and the American bases in the Philippines lay on the flank of any move south. Admiral Yamamoto Isoroku argued for a surprise blow that would win six months to a year of freedom of action. He also warned that Japan could not win a long war against American industry.

    Was war “forced” on Japan?

    The Japanese government said so in December 1941. But the US demand was clear: leave China. Tokyo could have accepted a costly retreat. The leaders chose war because withdrawing from China, after four years and huge losses, seemed politically impossible. You will test this claim yourself in the simulation (Task 5).

    05 · Perspectives · G4

    What historians argue

    Key idea: historians disagree mainly about two things: was there a plan, and who was responsible?

    Use named historians to support an argument, not as decoration. The summaries give each historian’s main line of argument in our own words. Full titles are in the reading list.

    ← Japan planned and chose aggressionJapan reacted to circumstances →

    The Tokyo Trial view

    International Military Tribunal for the Far East, 1946–1948

    Japan’s leaders conspired from 1928 onward to wage aggressive war and dominate Asia. Expansion was a plan, and its authors were criminals. Critics call the trial “victors’ justice” and point out that it shielded the Emperor for political reasons.

    Herbert Bix

    Hirohito and the Making of Modern Japan (2000)

    The Emperor was not a powerless figurehead. Hirohito was informed, took part in strategic decisions and approved the move to war. Responsibility goes to the top of the system.

    Louise Young

    Japan’s Total Empire (1998)

    Manchukuo was a popular project. Newspapers, radio, songs, business and settlers all invested in it. Expansion was driven from below as well as by the army; Japanese society was complicit.

    James Crowley and Michael Barnhart

    Japan’s Quest for Autonomy (1966) · Japan Prepares for Total War (1987)

    Policy came from a search for security and economic self-sufficiency. The lesson of the First World War was that modern wars need resources. The quest for autarky led Japan into the very dependence it feared.

    Akira Iriye

    The Origins of the Second World War in Asia and the Pacific (1987)

    Japan’s choices must be seen in an international system that broke down after 1929. Japan left the cooperative “Washington system” and tried to build a regional order of its own, which clashed with American ideas of the open door.

    Eri Hotta

    Japan 1941: Countdown to Infamy (2013)

    Many of Japan’s leaders doubted they could win, yet none was willing to take responsibility for stopping the drift to war. The decision of 1941 came from weak, divided leadership as much as from outside pressure.

    Japanese nationalist view

    Postwar right-wing writers and some politicians

    Japan fought a war of self-defence against Western encirclement and helped liberate Asia from colonial rule. Most historians, in Japan and abroad, reject this as an apology that ignores Japanese atrocities and colonial rule.

    Challenge Make two historians argue

    Write a short paragraph in which Bix and Young disagree about who was responsible for Japanese expansion. Then add a third sentence: which of them does the Mukden Incident support better?

    Theory of Knowledge link

    Estimates of the number of victims of the Nanjing Massacre (December 1937 to early 1938) range from tens of thousands to more than 300,000. Why might the same evidence produce such different figures? What does it mean for a historian to be “objective” about an event that is still part of national politics in China and Japan today?

    06 · Paper 1 practice · G4

    Source analysis

    Four sources, four question types, following the classic Paper 1 structure. Write your answer first. Then open the help or the model answer and use the checklist to judge your own work.

    Source A
    Yamagata Aritomo, Prime Minister of Japan, speech to the first session of the Imperial Diet, December 1890 (adapted translation).
    The independence and security of the nation depend first upon the protection of the line of sovereignty, and then upon the protection of the line of advantage. The line of sovereignty is the nation’s border; the line of advantage is the area closely related to the safety of that border. If we wish to maintain the nation’s independence, it is not enough to guard the line of sovereignty alone; we must also protect the line of advantage.
    Source B
    Report of the Commission of Enquiry of the League of Nations (the Lytton Report), published October 1932 (extracts).
    The military operations of the Japanese troops during this night [18 September 1931] cannot be regarded as measures of legitimate self-defence… The present régime [Manchukuo] cannot be considered to have been called into existence by a genuine and spontaneous independence movement.
    Source C
    Prince Konoe Fumimaro, Prime Minister of Japan, government statement on a “New Order in East Asia”, 3 November 1938 (adapted translation).
    What Japan seeks is the establishment of a new order which will ensure the permanent stability of East Asia. This new order has for its foundation a relationship of mutual aid between Japan, Manchukuo and China in political, economic and cultural fields. Japan does not reject the participation of the present Nationalist Government if it abandons its former policy and changes its personnel.
    Source D
    Imperial Rescript declaring war on the United States and the British Empire, issued in Tokyo on 8 December 1941 (adapted from the official English translation).
    It has been truly unavoidable and far from Our wishes that Our Empire has now been brought to cross swords with America and Britain… They have obstructed by every means Our peaceful commerce, and finally resorted to a direct severance of economic relations, menacing gravely the existence of Our Empire… Our Empire, for its existence and self-defence, has no other recourse but to appeal to arms.

    Need help? How to plan a “to what extent” essay
    • Your thesis must answer the question: “to a large extent”, “only partly”, “not at all”, and say why.
    • Each argument needs specific evidence (dates, names, numbers) and, if you can, a historian.
    • Show change over time: the answer for 1931 may differ from the answer for 1941.
    • The conclusion makes a judgement. It does not repeat your arguments.
    • Thesis starter: “Economic factors were important, especially in 1941, but …”

    Peer feedback: two stars and a wish

    Swap plans with a partner (paste them into the class chat, or show your screen). Use these questions on your partner’s plan:

    • Does the thesis say to what extent, and why?
    • Does every argument have specific evidence?
    • Is there a historian, used to support a point?
    • Does the plan show change between 1931 and 1941?
